# Agent Notch: make the human the plot twist

Research date: **13 October 2026**. Recommendation: make **“We replaced our slowest agent”**, a 55-second performance-review parody that exposes the viewer as the bottleneck, then proves the remedy in the real app. Spend first on the joke, readable product evidence and sound. Extra rendering quality comes later.

## Five distinct 45–60-second concepts

All scripts below are original proposals. Shared closing information: Agent Notch; free/open source/MIT; the Homebrew command; GitHub. Product behavior comes from the [local README](/Users/nikita/Projects/agent-notch-release/README.md), inspected 13 October. Each concept must show a human decision. A twenty-minute wait is a dramatized pre-install incident, not a claim that the app holds its hook indefinitely: the README specifies a 100-second fallback to the agent’s own window.

### 1. “We replaced our slowest agent” — recommended, 55 seconds

**Hook, 0–3:** a performance-review document slams onto screen: “WE REPLACED OUR SLOWEST AGENT.” Twelve worker records remain green; one record is amber.

**3–10:** rapid evidence montage: tests running, code changing, then one permission prompt frozen. Caption: “Waiting for approval. 20 minutes.” A discreet “Dramatization” label accompanies the invented incident.

**10–16:** the amber employee record rotates. Name: “YOU.” Cut to a human hand still holding coffee. Silence lets the accusation land.

**16–23:** “Keep the human. Fix the handoff.” Push into the physical notch; match-cut to the real amber mark and dropdown. Show “Agent Notch” here, before the final card.

**23–38:** readable demonstration: inspect a safe test command, press ⌃⌥Y, show the terminal resume. A different risky request receives a deliberate mouse-click Deny. No mass approval montage.

**38–47:** follow the original task to a completion toast; jump to its window. The performance document updates: “Available when needed.”

**47–55:** “Your agents were ready.” Product name, installation command held for five seconds, repository and license.

**Spread hypothesis:** viewers can quote the accusation, tag their distracted teammate, or object to it. The reversal also defuses fear of replacement. **Risk:** shaming users or implying autonomous approvals; make the human competent once the handoff is visible. **Production:** Claude writes Remotion’s report graphics, deterministic evidence montage and cuts; Blender supplies one 2–3-second notch push. Owner records the coffee hand and actual interaction. **UNVERIFIED estimate:** 18–26 working hours plus renders.

### 2. “The waiting room” — absurd office comedy, 52 seconds

**0–3:** twelve anonymous terminal-shaped workers sit beneath “WAITING FOR HUMAN.” Ticket display: “Now serving: nobody.”

**3–12:** one holds “Run tests?” Another carries an enormous folder marked “Plan approval.” A tiny subagent sits beside its parent.

**12–20:** a worker clocks out of the waiting room, walks one step, and clocks back in. Caption: “Your team has a manager problem.”

**20–28:** the queue-number display becomes a MacBook notch; match-cut into the real panel.

**28–41:** approve the test command, answer one question, show work resume. Each decision removes one waiting-room chair through a visual match cut.

**41–46:** completion toast; final worker says, “We thought you’d left.”

**46–52:** “Stop making your agents wait.” Install card.

**Why share:** a meme-sized office situation with recurring characters for later clips. **Risk:** cute animation can swallow the product; reveal it by second 20. Use original characters, never anthropomorphized vendor logos. **Needs:** Blender instances, simple pose loops and one room; Remotion labels, compositing and UI. **UNVERIFIED estimate:** 26–40 hours; highest character-production burden.

### 3. “Operation: Enter” — miniature heist, 55 seconds

**0–3:** a laser-filled vault opens onto one terminal button: “Allow.” Caption: “The whole operation depends on this.”

**3–13:** twelve worker identities and a plan board: code, test, review. Everything is ready; the last door stays locked.

**13–21:** stopwatch escalates. “Where’s the handler?” Cut to an innocent human working in another window.

**21–29:** amber light beside the notch. Camera dives through it into the real request.

**29–42:** human reads the command and approves; workers resume. A risky command appears as a second door; the human rejects it instead of opening everything.

**42–48:** original task completes. Freeze the team’s celebration into the completion toast.

**48–55:** “Keep the operation moving.” Installation and GitHub.

**Why share:** tension and an obvious mission make the UI action consequential. **Risk:** suggesting covert activity or unsafe automation. Keep the mission mundane—shipping a test fix—and use an original score. **Needs:** Blender vault/door/light rigs; Remotion identities and real screen inserts. **UNVERIFIED estimate:** 24–36 hours; lighting can consume the schedule.

### 4. “Approval League” — sports-broadcast parody, 50 seconds

**0–3:** oversized scoreboard: “AGENTS: 12 / APPROVALS: 0.” Announcer: “An incredible display of absolutely nothing.”

**3–14:** instant replay of a permission prompt waiting while the human changes windows. Broadcast arrow circles the missed prompt.

**14–23:** analyst: “The manager hasn’t seen the signal.” Cut to amber notch; “Agent Notch” score bug appears.

**23–36:** live UI: read command, ⌃⌥Y, terminal resumes. Replay the exact keypress once, with its real result.

**36–43:** one deliberate Deny: “Good decision.” Completion toast: “And they finish the task.”

**43–50:** “Better visibility. Better decisions.” Install card.

**Why share:** quotable commentary and a familiar broadcast grammar work as short clips. **Risk:** irritating voiceover, unreadable overlays, or copying a real league/network package. Invent the league, graphics and announcer voice. **Needs:** almost entirely Remotion; Blender optional. **UNVERIFIED estimate:** 14–22 hours; best inexpensive alternative.

### 5. “Something stopped” — horror fake-out, 54 seconds

**0–3:** terminal activity abruptly stops. An amber cursor blinks. Caption: “Something stopped your agent.”

**3–15:** a slow camera approach; clocks advance. Other windows conceal the prompt. Original low-frequency sound, no copied horror sting.

**15–23:** an amber silhouette seems threatening. Hard cut: it is simply “May I run the tests?” Caption: “It was you.”

**23–34:** warm lighting replaces cold; reveal Agent Notch and its waiting mark. Human inspects and approves.

**34–44:** the terminal runs; completion toast. The ominous pulse becomes a useful event sound.

**44–54:** “The scariest bug is the one waiting for you.” Local logs/no network, then install card.

**Why share:** suspense resolves into a painfully ordinary development problem. **Risk:** slow suspense loses feed viewers; red must remain reserved for actual errors, amber for waiting. **Needs:** Blender macro lighting and one push; Remotion window occlusion, captions and UI. **UNVERIFIED estimate:** 18–28 hours.

## Distribution is part of the edit

The following lengths are editorial starting points, not platform limits or proven optima.

| Surface | Deliverable | Opening and purpose |
|---|---|---|
| X | 55-second master, 16:9; separate 20–25-second 4:5 cut | Performance-review accusation immediately; post native video, with a useful product link in accompanying copy. |
| YouTube | 55-second 16:9 master; 25–35-second 9:16 Short | Master explains the reversal; Short follows just one missed approval. |
| TikTok/Reels | 20–30-second 9:16 cut | Put the human reveal by second 5, the app by second 8; enlarge one request, not a whole desktop. |
| Product Hunt | Master via YouTube plus real UI gallery images | Comedy earns interest; the gallery explains actual behavior and setup. |
| HN/Reddit | Repository/app first; optional 12–20-second demonstration | Lead with the concrete missed-prompt problem and implementation details, then discuss limitations. |

[TikTok’s June 2025 guidance](https://ads.tiktok.com/resources/help/article/creative-best-practices?lang=en) recommends stating the proposition within three seconds, prioritizing the hook within six, and using captions. [YouTube’s 3 October 2024 announcement](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/tall-updates-coming-to-shorts/) allows Shorts up to three minutes from 15 October; that ceiling is not a reason to fill it. [X documents native autoplay](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/x-videos). Design for muted viewing: burn in dialogue and essential labels, retain useful sound for those who enable it, and test captions against each platform’s controls. Never require audio to understand the reversal.

[Product Hunt’s guide](https://www.producthunt.com/launch/preparing-for-launch) supports YouTube links, advises showing the product, and discourages asking for upvotes. [Show HN rules](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html) exclude landing pages and promotional material from Show HN: submit the usable app, with film secondary. Both checked 13 October. Verify each subreddit’s current rules before posting; this report does not establish permission to promote there.

Before launch, show the exported cuts muted on an actual phone to five target developers. Ask what the app does, who approves, and what they would click next. This is a proposed qualitative check, not a statistical experiment. After launch compare retention and repository visits, separating paid reach from organic sharing. High comment volume can be hostility rather than intent to install.

## Top 10 recommendations for Agent Notch

Ranked by my expected impact per working hour; estimates are **UNVERIFIED**, and $0 means no additional software purchase, excluding existing subscriptions and labor.

1. **Commit to the performance-review fake-out.** Why: one memorable accusation. How: write and time a rough 55-second animatic first. Cost: $0, 2–3 hours. Risk: insulting the audience; resolve it with competent human control.
2. **Show one real permission-to-resumption sequence.** Why: earns developer trust. How: capture the native panel, safe approval and terminal result. Cost: $0, 2–4 hours. Risk: private logs; use a controlled demo project.
3. **Delete the launch-film feature inventory.** Why: preserves narrative momentum. How: keep visibility, decision, completion; move settings to follow-ups. Cost: $0, 1–2 hours. Risk: some capabilities need separate explanation.
4. **Make the first frame tell the story.** Why: works during scrolling and previews. How: readable accusation plus amber worker record. Cost: $0, 1 hour. Risk: a replacement hook can mislead if the reversal arrives too late.
5. **Author vertical cuts separately.** Why: the notch is tiny on a phone. How: new camera/layout, single-request crop, earlier payoff. Cost: $0, 3–5 hours. Risk: cropped context obscures the action.
6. **Build captions for silent comprehension.** Why: the joke must survive mute. How: short phrases, timed holds and platform-overlay checks. Cost: $0, 1–2 hours. Risk: captions compete with command text.
7. **Use silence at “YOU,” then a purposeful sound return.** Why: makes the reversal felt. How: edit stems and event sounds around the decision. Cost: $0, 2–3 hours. Risk: exaggerated alerts sound exhausting.
8. **Buy voice only if the joke needs delivery.** Why: deadpan timing can improve the performance review or sports version. How: owner performance first; ElevenLabs paid TTS if weaker. Cost: $6/month, 1–2 hours. Risk: generic synthetic delivery and licensing conditions.
9. **Limit Blender to one meaningful notch shot.** Why: establishes the physical product without slowing it. How: a short match-cut push with real UI composited through existing corner data. Cost: $0, 3–5 hours plus render. Risk: geometry polish displaces story work.
10. **Release a proof-led launch package.** Why: viewers need somewhere credible to go. How: master, short cut, clear poster, readable install card, repository and setup note. Cost: $0, 2–3 hours. Risk: views fail to convert if installation friction is hidden.
